How government does forecasting — and how it checks itself

A cited landscape of forecasting, projection, and retrospective evaluation across government bodies. Thesis Institute, July 2026. Every load-bearing claim carries a primary citation checked in this research pass; live-site and repository numbers are as of the evening of 2026-07-30 (US Eastern) and move daily.

Summary

Government forecasting is evaluated more than its critics assume and less rigorously than its defenders imply, and the gap between those two facts is where the case for an external evaluation layer lives.

The self-evaluation that exists is real. CBO publishes an annual accuracy report about three months after the fiscal year closes. The OBR is required by statute to assess its own forecast accuracy every financial year, and its June 2026 report states that it underestimated 2024-25 borrowing by £65 billion at the one-year horizon. OMB has published a statutory prior-year comparison for decades. IRS, FAA, NCES, USDA, CMS, Washington State, and Virginia all print their own error statistics. Any argument that begins “government never checks itself” is false and will be answered with documents.

The real finding is narrower and holds only where it applies. Among the fiscal and macroeconomic institutions reviewed, and across the sampled program-agency workload and caseload products, no standing published evaluation uses a proper scoring rule — every metric found is mean error, absolute error, RMSE, signed differential, or narrative attribution, none of which can detect overconfidence. Two corners of the same government are the explicit exceptions, and any statement of this finding has to name them or it is false: NWS verifies precipitation probabilities with the Brier score, and CDC’s hubs score quantile submissions with the weighted interval score. Both are government program forecasting. Probability distributions are published (IMF growth bands with explicit tail probabilities, Fed and OBR fan charts) and no standing public evaluation of them was found — though delayed Tealbooks show the Fed conducting recurring internal interval review; the most self-critical institution in the set made isolated outturn-versus-band observations in two located editions (2016 and 2024), with no recurring coverage-rate series found.

That claim is bounded by what a forecast is. A CBO or JCT cost estimate is a conditional causal estimate against a statutory baseline: only one of its two worlds ever occurs, so it cannot be resolved against an outcome at all, and demanding that it be scored is a category error rather than a critique. The taxonomy below separates the products where scoring applies from those where it does not, and every finding here is scoped accordingly.

Weather and outbreak forecasting demonstrate the design for repeated, short-horizon, observable targets. Whether it transfers to fiscal estimation is unproven, and this report does not claim otherwise. The federal experiments that tried adjacent versions produced uneven results and then ended: ACE is the clear success, HFC’s published gain over a recruited-crowd comparator was modest and never isolated the machine contribution, and SCORE’s human methods showed signal while its automated objective fell short. INFER, the survivor, is now the RAND Forecasting Initiative, with no continuing federal owner or appropriation identified.

Thesis’s contribution is architectural: resolution rules and target vintages registered before the run, first-print resolution, externally witnessed custody, and CRPS with interval coverage and PIT, across a 750-target registry concentrated in the operational program layer. That architecture does not yet cover its own catalog — 95 targets carry prospective registration snapshots against 379 backfilled ledger entries, and full artifact traces exist for 351 of 1,188 runs (July 30 export). Its witnessed scoreboard reads eight scored forecasts, three of them inside their stated 80% intervals — 38% coverage against an 80% target — and on the two persistence-matched targets the agent is currently behind the baseline. Eight observations support no inference about accuracy in either direction; what the machinery demonstrates is that forecasts resolve and publish themselves, in both directions, without an intervening decision about whether to report them. It scores its own forecasts, not anyone else’s, so it is not yet an external evaluator of the institutions described here.

How to read this document

Three kinds of statements appear below, and the distinction is the method:

  1. Observed — read directly from a primary document or live surface this pass (agency PDFs, statutes, committee reports, app.thesisinstitute.org and the public ThesisInstitute/thesis repository). These carry citations.
  2. Reported — a secondary source’s characterization (GAO on agencies, press on program status). Attributed as such.
  3. Absence claims (“no agency does X”) — the searches that failed to find a counterexample are documented in the underlying research notes and fetch logs, retained and available on request. Absence of evidence is labeled as exactly that.

For each body covered: what it forecasts, at what frequency and horizon; whether forecasts are ever resolved against outcomes; whether scoring is proper (interval/CRPS-family) or narrative/point-error; whether anything is pre-registered or tamper-evident; and cost or latency where documented.

What can and cannot be scored

Government publishes four different kinds of statements about the future, and they are not interchangeable. A criticism that is devastating for one is a category error for another, so every claim in this report is scoped to a type.

Type A — probability distributions over later-observed quantities. IMF growth bands, Fed SEP fan charts, CDC hub quantile submissions, NWS precipitation probabilities. These can be scored properly (CRPS, WIS, Brier) and their interval coverage can be tested. Scoring applies fully.

Type B — point forecasts of later-observed quantities. CBO’s economic forecasts and baseline aggregates, WASDE commodity projections, IRS filing volumes, FAA enplanements, state revenue estimates, SNAP participation, the Trustees’ near-term income and cost. These resolve against observable outcomes and their error can be measured — but a point forecast cannot be assessed for calibration unless a distribution is elicited. Resolution applies; proper scoring applies only if the forecaster is asked for a distribution.

Type C — conditional causal estimates against a stipulated baseline. A CBO or JCT cost estimate states: budget outcome with this bill minus budget outcome under statutory baseline conventions. Only one of those worlds occurs. The counterfactual is never observed; later laws intervene, provisions interact, behavioral responses are endogenous. A proper score cannot cure a missing counterfactual. Regulatory benefit-cost estimates and Treasury’s revenue-proposal estimates share this structure. Direct resolution is generally impossible. What can be evaluated is separable observable components — a projected participation count, a take-up rate, a repatriation volume — and only where the component can be isolated.

Type D — scenarios and sensitivity paths. The Trustees’ low-cost and high-cost alternatives, FAA’s optimistic and pessimistic cases, CMS’s illustrative alternative. Most carry no probabilities and are deliberately not predictions. Without probabilities they cannot be proper-scored or read as nominal coverage intervals, though agencies can and do compare their paths and assumptions against realized history — EIA’s AEO retrospective review does exactly that. Washington’s ERFC is the instructive exception: it assigns 50% to its baseline, 20% to the optimistic case, and 30% to the pessimistic one, which makes those forecasts Type A in form — and no CRPS, log score, or calibration series is published for them.

The findings below bite on Types A and B, where outcomes are observable and scoring is available but unused. On Type C the honest claim is much narrower: not that CBO and JCT should score bills, but that separable observable components are rarely evaluated, and that the conditional structure tends to be lost in transmission — an estimate gets quoted as a prediction long after its baseline caveats have fallen away. Type D appears for completeness, not indictment.

1. Fiscal scorekeepers

Congressional Budget Office

What and how much. Under the Congressional Budget Act of 1974, CBO produces the baseline budget projections Congress scores against and, in its own words, “between 600 and 800 cost estimates for bills ordered reported to the Congress each year,” plus “about 350 additional estimates” for House suspension bills — roughly 950-1,150 published estimates a year, not the “thousands” sometimes claimed (CBO cost-estimates FAQ). Revenue and direct-spending provisions are estimated over 10 years; discretionary authorizations over 5. For tax legislation, the Budget Act requires CBO to incorporate JCT’s estimates for legislation involving the Internal Revenue Code. Some baseline conventions are statutory — expiring programs with outlays over $50 million assumed to continue, entitlement authority assumed fully funded. Others are CBO practice: the agency “generally assumes” a 50% probability for pending administrative actions, one of the few explicit probabilities anywhere in the process, used as a scenario weight and never scored.

The accuracy machinery — real, aggregate, and point-error. CBO is the best self-evaluator among the scorekeepers. Each winter it publishes The Accuracy of CBO’s Budget Projections — the FY2025 edition arrived January 16, 2026, about 3.5 months after the fiscal year closed (CBO pub. 61916). It exists “at the request of the House and Senate Committees on the Budget” — not by statute. The FY2025 numbers: revenues underestimated by 6% ($334 billion on the June 2024 baseline), outlays by 2%, deficit overestimated by 0.7% of GDP. Historical benchmarks from the same report: average absolute budget-year error of 1.1% of GDP on deficits (1985-2024), 6% on revenues, 3% on outlays — and 18% on corporate income taxes. FY2025’s customs-duties error was 61%, because the 2025 tariff actions were administrative and therefore outside the baseline; CBO’s convention counts such surprises “as projection errors because they can be difficult to separate from other effects.”

What is not evaluated at the provision level. The accuracy report adjusts its baselines for subsequently enacted legislation using the original cost estimates, with the stated consequence that “any errors in the initial cost estimates are reflected in the projection errors discussed in this report” — carried into the aggregate rather than attributed to any individual bill. The FY2025 edition breaks out exactly one piece of legislation (a student-loan provision of P.L. 119-21 that came in $23 billion under the estimate).

The reason is structural, not evasive, and the report should say so plainly: a cost estimate is a Type C conditional causal estimate. Once a bill is enacted, the no-bill world it was measured against never happens. CBO’s conventions exist so that Congress can compare competing texts consistently under deadline, not as predictions about what will ultimately occur. Where a component is separable — a participation count, a take-up rate — ex-post evaluation is feasible, and that is the narrow place where more evaluation is available rather than impossible.

The economic record. CBO’s Economic Forecasting Record: 2025 Update (July 14, 2025, pub. 61334) scores two- and five-year forecast averages against outcomes using mean absolute error and RMSE, and benchmarks them: CBO’s two-year forecasts of output, CPI inflation, and the 10-year Treasury rate were “11 percent more accurate than the Administration, 4 percent more accurate than the Blue Chip consensus, and 7 percent more accurate than the SPF, on average.” The metrics are explicitly point-error; no probability distribution is elicited or scored anywhere in either report. CBO publishes historical-error-based uncertainty ranges for selected economic variables and, separately, deterministic rules-of-thumb scenarios (How Changes in Economic Conditions Might Affect the Federal Budget, pub. 62373); no ex-post evaluation of interval coverage accompanies either.

Vintage integrity. Old projections stay archived, and CBO now maintains a public, git-versioned repository of its evaluation data (US-CBO/eval-projections) — genuine transparency infrastructure, though nothing is timestamped or witnessed beyond ordinary publication.

Joint Committee on Taxation

JCT produces the official revenue estimates for tax legislation, which the Budget Act requires CBO to incorporate. Its ex-post record is the sharpest absence in this report, and JCT states the reason itself.

No systematic public retrospective exists. JCT’s own methodology publication says it plainly: “Although there is no formal process for the revisiting of estimates over time” (JCX-46-11, pp. 6-7), economists update models annually as new evidence arrives. The document attributes the gap to real difficulties — the counterfactual is unobserved, later law changes intervene, provisions interact, and econometric attribution is costly and contestable. Its 1992 methodology discussion (JCS-14-92, p. 21) similarly describes accuracy monitoring folded into ordinary model updating, for isolated provisions where data permit. That is internal recalibration, not a published scorecard.

What exists instead is a handful of one-off comparisons, and they are worth knowing precisely because they show what a real retrospective would have to overcome:

  • JGTRRA (JCX-19-06, pp. 15-16): actual FY2003-2005 receipts came in $93 billion below the combined CBO baseline plus JCT estimate — with JCT itself warning that intervening 2004 legislation made the comparison problematic.
  • CARES economic impact payments (JCT presentation, January 2025, p. 16): $269 billion expected to be paid by September 30, 2020 against Treasury’s actual $275 billion. The same slide records that JCT adjusted its model using the estimated-versus-actual gap from the 2008 stimulus payments — direct evidence that internal ex-post calibration happens without ever becoming a public score.
  • The 2004 repatriation holiday: JCT projected roughly $235 billion in qualifying dividends; IRS data showed $312 billion, about 33% higher. The analysis was published in Tax Notes by Edward Kleinbard and Patrick Driessen — then JCT’s chief of staff and a senior economist — expressly as the authors’ views, not the committee’s.

JCT publishes no recurring provision-level retrospective series; the public material consists of a few bounded comparisons where later data permitted one, plus the internal recalibration its own documents describe. Its stated reasons are the Type C reasons — unobserved counterfactual, interacting provisions, endogenous behavior — and they are legitimate. The gap worth naming is not that JCT declines to score bills, which often cannot be done, but that no public record exists of which separable components it checked, what it found, or how models changed as a result.

Office of Management and Budget

OMB’s economic assumptions (built with CEA and Treasury) underpin the President’s Budget on a 10-year horizon, refreshed at mid-session. Two standing retrospectives exist, both point-error, both aggregate:

  • Analytical Perspectives ch. 1, “Forecast Errors.” The FY2027 edition’s Table 01-4 reports average error, mean absolute error, and RMSE against CBO and Blue Chip (Table 01-2 separately compares current projections with CBO, the FOMC, and Blue Chip) — and states the historical pattern plainly: “For the budget year, however, the historical pattern has been for the budget to understate the deficit by an average of 0.7 percent of GDP” (Analytical Perspectives, FY2027 Budget, ch. 1, 2026).
  • Analytical Perspectives ch. 14, “Comparison of Actual to Estimated Totals” — the statutory retrospective. 31 U.S.C. §1105(a) requires the budget to compare prior-year estimated versus actual receipts and outlays; ch. 14 of the FY2027 edition does so for FY2025 (initial deficit estimate $1,865 billion; receipts came in $150 billion higher and outlays $59 billion higher). The comparison appeared 753 days after the March 11, 2024 budget it evaluates, and about six months after FY2025 closed. No intervals, no probabilistic scoring, no per-proposal resolution.

2025-26 status, observed. A full Analytical Perspectives volume shipped with the FY2027 budget (April 2026). The FY2026 Mid-Session Review appeared as a “Technical Supplement to the 2026 Budget,” after the statutory deadline (it cites CBO’s July 21, 2025 OBBBA estimate; 31 U.S.C. §1106 requires submission “[b]efore July 16”). As of July 27, 2026, no FY2027 Mid-Session Review — due before July 16, 2026 — appears on OMB’s budget page.

Treasury Office of Tax Analysis

OTA estimates the revenue effects of Administration proposals (the “Greenbook”) and the receipts baseline. Two observed facts define its accountability posture:

  • The Greenbook is optional in practice. Treasury’s Revenue Proposals index lists editions for FY1991-FY2017 and FY2022-FY2025 — none for FY2018-FY2021, and none for FY2026 or FY2027 as of July 27, 2026. When no Greenbook is published, Administration tax proposals carry no published proposal-level Treasury estimates at all.
  • The last published self-analysis of Treasury’s forecast record found in this pass is from 1987 — OTA Paper 58 (George A. Plesko, “Government Forecasts and Budget Projections: An Analysis of Recent History,” October 1987), a staff paper stamped “do not reflect Treasury policy.” Nothing on the OTA working-paper index since then evaluates receipts-estimate accuracy.

State consensus revenue estimating

Twenty-eight states produce their official revenue estimate through a consensus process — a group formally representing both the executive and legislative branches — against 12 using separate executive and legislative forecasts and 10 relying on the executive alone (NASBO, Budget Processes in the States, Spring 2021, Figure 3 and Table 6, pp. 37-38; NASBO updates this survey roughly every five years, and searches located no newer state edition as of July 2026). Most states release estimates two or three times a year; seven states plus DC release four or more.

The mechanics are worth seeing because they are a genuine institutional answer to forecast manipulation — one that does not depend on scoring. Florida’s Revenue Estimating Conference produces the state’s “official information” on anticipated revenues under Fla. Stat. §216.136(3), and §216.133(3) defines consensus as the unanimous consent of all principals — professional staff for the governor, the Senate, the House, and the legislature’s Office of Economic and Demographic Research. Meetings are public, any principal can withhold agreement, the forecast assumes current law under §216.134 with a statutory minimum ten-year horizon, and §216.137 requires a post-session revision and continuous monitoring. Michigan runs January and May conferences on a similar model; Washington’s forecast council is an independent agency outside both branches.

Consensus is an ex ante legitimacy device: it makes the number hard to push around before it is published. It is not an accuracy mechanism, and the distinction matters for this report — unanimity among principals says nothing about whether the agreed number turns out to be right. What states do about accuracy afterward varies widely and is covered in section 8.

Why the scorekeepers are built this way

Three constraints explain most of what looks like missing accountability, and a report that ignores them will not be taken seriously by the people who work inside these institutions.

Estimates are enforcement instruments before they are predictions. Budget Committees use CBO and JCT estimates in scorekeeping, and the applicable committee’s determination governs Budget Act points of order (2 U.S.C. §643); the same estimates decide whether a reconciliation bill satisfies its instructions and whether statutory PAYGO is triggered. Congress needs one official, comparable number produced under consistent conventions, on deadline, for competing versions of a text. The statutory baseline conventions — expiring programs assumed to continue, entitlement authority assumed fully funded — exist to make texts comparable and to keep the scorekeeper neutral, not because anyone believes they predict what Congress will do. Judged as predictions they will often be wrong by construction; that is the design working.

Being scored is politically live in both directions. Members already engineer sunsets, phase-ins, effective dates, and offsets to land a score inside a window, and misses get weaponized. A public accuracy leaderboard would discipline some of that gaming, and would also hand a ready-made cudgel to whoever wants to discredit an independent CBO or JCT in a given week. Any proposal for external scoring has to reckon with both effects; this report does not treat the political economy as settled.

Forecasts here are endogenous and sometimes self-negating. Fed projections condition on a policy path that responds to the outlook. Fiscal forecasts prompt the legislation that supersedes them. The Trustees’ depletion dates are published precisely to provoke the reform that would falsify them. A raw outcome score can penalize a successful warning, so any serious evaluation must separate model error, condition failure, policy response, and implementation — which is harder than scoring and is part of why it is rare.

2. Macroeconomic forecasters

Federal Reserve: the SEP and the Tealbook lag

Correcting the lag claim first. The Summary of Economic Projections is a same-day release, four times a year: GDP growth, unemployment, PCE and core PCE inflation, and the federal funds rate path, with central tendencies and full ranges, published the afternoon the FOMC meeting ends. What carries a roughly five-year lag is the staff forecast — the Tealbook — released inside the historical FOMC materials package. Participant-level SEP files are also delayed five years, with identity keys released at five years for March 2016 onward and ten years for 2007-2015. The distinction matters: the projections themselves are contemporaneous.

Uncertainty is published; SEP-specific accuracy is not scored. The SEP carries an error-range table and fan charts, but their construction matters: they are generic pooled historical errors of various forecasters over about twenty years, following the methodology of Reifschneider and Tulip (FEDS 2017-020) — not the realized errors of past SEP vintages. A one-off check in that 2017 paper did compute errors for median SEP projections from 2007-2015, and a 2026 staff paper (Engstrom, FEDS 2026-026) finds the rate dots beat several survey and market comparators, but both are authored research that “does not indicate Board concurrence.” No standing Board program resolves each SEP vintage against outcomes, publishes an evaluation calendar, or scores participants. The defensible formulation is no standing program found — the searches supporting that absence are documented in the companion notes.

The reform window opened and closed. The 2025 framework review explicitly considered SEP changes — the June 2025 minutes record discussion of alternative scenarios; at the review’s research conference Ben Bernanke proposed a quarterly staff-led forecast, in-meeting SEP updates, and de-emphasizing the medians. Asked in March 2026 what came of it, Chair Powell said the Committee found no idea with sufficiently broad support and “didn’t really make any changes” (FOMC press conference transcript, March 18, 2026, pp. 14-15). The institution examined its own forecast-communication accountability at the highest level and elected to keep it as is.

Vintage integrity. Real-time PDFs, dated release notices, and the later historical packages make an unusually strong public archive — and no cryptographic hash, trusted timestamp, or append-only log anywhere in it. “Archived” and “tamper-evident” are different claims, and only the first is earned here.

UK Office for Budget Responsibility: the accountability high-water mark

The OBR earns the title, and the precise version is more useful than the loose one.

What the statute actually says. Section 4(4)(a) of the Budget Responsibility and National Audit Act 2011 requires the OBR to prepare, on at least one occasion each financial year, “an assessment of the accuracy of fiscal and economic forecasts previously prepared by it,” and s.4(5) requires that assessment to appear in a report. That is the whole duty. The law does not name the Forecast Evaluation Report, mandate a standalone publication, or prescribe the error decomposition — and s.6(2) actually bars the Charter from prescribing the OBR’s methods. The current Charter (Autumn 2025) says only that “[t]he OBR will also produce analysis of past forecasting performance.” So the correct formulation is: the annual accuracy assessment is statutory; the FER’s format and its four-way decomposition are OBR practice. Anyone citing a “statutory annual FER with statutory error decomposition” is overstating it.

The latest edition and what it evaluates. The Forecast evaluation report — June 2026 (published 2 June 2026, 57pp) assesses forecasts for financial year 2024-25 at three horizons: the March 2024 EFO (one year), the March 2023 EFO (two years), and — because March 2020 predated the first lockdown and was not a meaningful pandemic forecast — the July 2020 Fiscal Sustainability Report central scenario stands in at five years, a comparator the OBR expressly says was not an official forecast. Coverage runs well beyond headline series: real and nominal GDP, the deflator, CPI, employment and earnings, public sector net borrowing, receipts, spending, debt, and underlying tax and spending lines, with the wider variable set in online annexes. Crucially, the “outturn” is a named, revisable vintage — the ONS first quarterly estimate of 12 February 2026 for the economy, the Public Sector Finances release of 23 April 2026 for the fiscal side.

The findings, which are not flattering and are published anyway. 2024-25 public sector net borrowing came in at £152 billion (5.2% of GDP). The two-year-ahead forecast said £85 billion — £66 billion low; the one-year-ahead forecast said £87 billion — £65 billion low. Real GDP growth was close (1.5% actual against 1.2% one year out), but nominal GDP growth of 5.5% was undershot by 3.6 percentage points, of which 3.2 points came from the GDP deflator. The report decomposes the fiscal misses into economic determinants (which raised receipts £68 billion and £20 billion against the two forecasts) and post-forecast policy changes (which added £32 billion and £26 billion to spending). It also compares OBR errors against the median of external forecasters in HM Treasury’s Forecasts for the UK economy — at two years, PSNB errors of 2.3% of GDP for the OBR against 2.5% for the external median.

Where the evaluation stops. The 2026 FER contains no fan-chart coverage test and no proper score: a full-document search finds no “coverage,” “Brier,” or “CRPS.” The quantitative content is signed point error plus narrative attribution — including a residual category defined as “any remaining differences that cannot be explained by the other categories.” Fan-chart bands are compared with outturns occasionally rather than systematically: the 2024 FER noted that “CPI inflation in 2023 was 7.3 per cent, which fell outside the fan chart’s 80 per cent probability bands,” and the 2016 FER located a borrowing outturn in the June 2010 fan chart’s tail. Individual hindsight observations, not a coverage series.

Cost and latency, documented. The OBR runs on a £6.372 million delegated budget with 55 staff in post at 31 March 2026 (£5.990 million actual net expenditure in 2025-26) — the whole office, not the FER alone; no per- evaluation cost is published. The June 2026 FER assessed financial year 2024-25 428 days after that year ended, and 818 days after the March 2024 EFO it evaluates. That lag is deliberate: the 2025 redesign traded speed for final or near-final rather than provisional outturn data.

Cadence, corrected. The IMF recommended one fiscal event per year, not one forecast, and the UK landed somewhere more interesting than either telling. As of July 2026 the normal pattern is two five-year EFO forecasts per financial year — a Budget forecast carrying the annual fiscal-rule assessment, and a spring interim forecast without it — but one major fiscal-policy event. Finance Act 2026 s.254 (Royal Assent 18 March 2026) moved the rule assessment to once yearly; notably, the Chancellor commissioned the 3 March 2026 Spring Forecast fifteen days before that provision became law, and the OBR proceeded on the “reasonable assumption” that the bill would pass, undertaking to comply with then-current law if it did not.

IMF World Economic Outlook

The WEO covers a far wider surface than the scan credited — many macro, fiscal, and external series across 197 economies, with four outlook releases a year but only two full database vintages (January and July are partial).

The WEO publishes genuine probability bands. April 2026’s Chapter 1 carries “Forecast Uncertainty around Global Growth and Inflation Projections” (Figure 1.3.1 and Box 1.3, pp. 25-26): distributions through 2031 in which each shaded band holds five percentage points of probability mass, generated from the IMF’s G20 model with the downside tilted to match the Global Financial Stability Report’s growth-at-risk assessment. It states explicit tail probabilities — a 25% chance global growth falls below 2% and a 38% chance global headline inflation exceeds 5%, on the horizon the figure covers. So “no intervals” is simply wrong for the IMF. What is missing is the other half: no standing published ex-post scoring of those bands — no recurring coverage series, no Brier, log, or CRPS scorecard — was located.

Evaluation is layered and episodic. The formal independent evaluation remains the IEO’s 2014 IMF Forecasts: Process, Quality, and Country Perspectives, with an implemented action calling for commissioned studies every five to eight years; an internal country-level point-error review tool has operated since the October 2017 WEO cycle. The best-known accuracy finding in this literature concerns turning points rather than bands: across 153 recessions in 63 countries (1992-2014), “[i]n April of the year before the recession, forecasters expected output to fall in only 5 of these 153 cases” (An, Jalles & Loungani, “How Well Do Economists Forecast Recessions?”, IMF Working Paper WP/18/39, 2018, p. 6). The IMF-specific table shows 147 of 153 missed at that horizon, with private forecasters showing a very similar pattern — a stringent diagnostic of recognition, not a probability score, and it should be quoted as such.

3. Program actuaries

Social Security: OCACT and the Trustees Report

The Trustees Report projects OASDI finances over 75 years under three scenarios (low-cost, intermediate, high-cost) with sensitivity analyses and stochastic simulations. Two distinct retrospective mechanisms sit inside it, and conflating them is the standard error:

A real point-error table. Table III.A4 of the 2026 report compares actual calendar-2025 income and cost with the intermediate estimates from each of the 2021-2025 reports, reporting signed percentage differences. Combined OASDI income was underestimated by 8.4% in the 2021 vintage, 4.4% in 2022, 2.1% in 2023, 2.0% in 2024, and 1.5% in 2025 — a formal, annually repeated scorecard covering roughly four-year-ahead through same-year estimates. It resolves one target year against two aggregates: no averaging across target years, no benchmark comparison, no MAE or RMSE, no probabilistic score.

Drift tracking, which is not scoring. Appendix B’s “History of Actuarial Status Estimates” tracks what each vintage since 1982 reported for the 75-year actuarial balance and the hypothetical depletion date — the 2022-2026 reports show 2035 → 2034 → 2035 → 2034 → 2034 — and explains movements through legislation, new data, assumption changes, and the rolling valuation window. This is genuine transparency about how the estimate moved. It is not resolution against a realized outcome, because the outcome has not occurred and will not for a decade. No recurring agency-run score of the 75-year Social Security or Medicare solvency projections was found — but “never scored anywhere in government” would be wrong, and the counterexample is worth knowing (see PBGC below).

Episodic self-evaluation. OCACT has published retrospectives — Actuarial Note 156, “Accuracy of Mortality Projections in Trustees Reports” (May 2015), comparing life-expectancy projections across vintages from 1982, and Note 157, comparing Trustees, CBO, and presidential GDP projections (noting none anticipated the Great Recession). Both are largely graphical and narrative. No OCACT-authored recurring proper score or statistically defined coverage series was found; the coverage test that exists is the external one below, against an envelope that was never a confidence interval.

The external audit, stated precisely. Kashin, King & Soneji, “Systematic Bias and Nontransparency in US Social Security Administration Forecasts,” Journal of Economic Perspectives 29(2):239-258 (2015), with a companion in Political Analysis 23(3):336-362, found errors roughly centered through 1999 but persistently signed after 2000 in the direction that made Social Security look healthier than it turned out to be: mean cost-rate residuals of +0.10 percentage points for 1978-1999 versus −1.38 after 2000 at the five-year horizon. Two qualifications bound this finding: their finding covers one- to ten-year point errors, not 75-year solvency; and the “intervals too narrow” result tests the span between Alternatives I and III, an envelope that (as the authors themselves note, and the Trustees disclaim) “does not have a formal statistical basis” — it was never a confidence interval to begin with. Peter Diamond further objected that common baseline errors cancel in policy scores, which limits their policy-score inference without touching the signed-error findings.

And the response. No OCACT document located names or cites either 2015 paper. Actuarial Note 150 (January 2013) rebuts an earlier King-Soneji New York Times op-ed; Notes 156 and 157 (May 2015) offer substantively related mortality and financial defenses — turning points, a roughly four-year data lag, a recession nobody forecast — without citing the journal articles or reproducing their residual analysis. OCACT directly answered the 2013 precursor op-ed; its 2015 notes offer related arguments, but no matched response to the journal papers was located. The dispute remains live and unmatched: the critique published replicable vintage residuals, and no reanalysis of those residuals was published in reply.

CMS Office of the Actuary

OACT produces the Medicare Trustees projections (75 years) and the National Health Expenditure projections (about ten years; the 2025-34 vintage appeared in Health Affairs 45(7):722-733, July 2026, projecting nearly $9.0 trillion, 20.6% of GDP, by 2034). Three things distinguish it:

The actuaries publish their own doubts about the baseline. Each year OACT issues an illustrative-alternative memo modeling what happens if current-law payment updates prove unsustainable. The June 9, 2026 edition (Shatto & Clemens) states the point directly: “an evaluation of the financial status of Medicare that is based on the provisions of current law is likely to portray an overly optimistic outcome.” Under the alternative, the 75-year HI actuarial deficit is 1.38% of taxable payroll rather than 0.56%. This is a government actuary publicly labeling the conditionality of its own headline number — the closest thing in the fiscal landscape to a self-issued reliability warning.

A standing annual comparison inside the Medicare report. The 2026 Medicare Trustees Report compares actual 2025 HI, Part B, and Part D amounts against the estimates in both the 2024 and 2025 reports — a prompt point comparison, about five months after year-end, and not a proper score.

A separate agency-authored accuracy analysis. “Analysis of National Health Expenditure Projections Accuracy” (November 2020) evaluates nine annual vintages against later historical estimates, reporting average signed growth-rate differentials by horizon: NHE projections ran +0.1 percentage points at year 1, +0.5 at year 2, and +1.0 at years 3-5, with Medicaid the worst at +2.3 over years 3-5. Because the statistic is signed, positive and negative errors cancel — it measures bias, not accuracy — and the table stops at year 5 despite the projections running ten.

And a versioning problem worth noting precisely. That accuracy analysis still sits beside the current projections as of July 27, 2026, resolving vintages only through historical year 2018. Its own footnote cites a February 2018 predecessor at the identical ProjectionAccuracy.pdf URL that now serves the November 2020 file. This review documents a version-retrieval and reproducibility problem — the predecessor is hard to obtain — and does not document alteration of any forecast vintage or score. Ordinary publication carries real practical safeguards: releases are downloaded by Congress, press, researchers, and libraries, and CBO maintains versioned evaluation data in public git. The narrow, defensible point is that publication chronology rests on those informal safeguards rather than on any cryptographic record.

PBGC: the one federal actuary whose distributions have been checked

The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation’s PIMS model produces stochastic projections — actual probability distributions, not scenario bands — and is the closest federal actuarial example of a long-horizon distribution receiving an episodic external backtest. Locating an outcome inside a percentile distribution is a backtest, not a Brier, log, or CRPS score.

An SSA-funded external review (Brown, Elliott, Gordon & Hammond, hosted by PBGC) compared the only five then-mature ten-year vintages (1998-2002) against realized deficits and found that actual deficits fell in the worst 10% of simulations for 1998-2001, with 2002 within one standard deviation but nearly $20 billion worse than the mean — the reviewers cautioning about the tiny sample, the recession, and intervening law changes. An earlier 2012 Buck review found 2009 multiemployer contributions projected from 2007 data averaged 23% above actual.

The institutional part is the more telling half. MAP-21 requires annual independent peer review of PIMS, and McKinsey’s July 2024 reviews still found “no formal process defined for back testing”; the May 2025 Athena review again recommended annual backtesting and holdout validation. So: a federal agency publishing genuine predictive distributions, with external reviewers repeatedly asking for a backtesting process, and none established. That is a sharper illustration of the gap than any absence claim — the capability is present, the distributions exist, and the loop still isn’t closed.

The technical-panel review model

The review mechanism is real but has lapsed, and the correction matters because “reviewed by technical panels” is doing load-bearing work in the scan’s framing. The Social Security Advisory Board’s Technical Panel on Assumptions and Methods convened in 1999, 2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, and 2019 (plus a differently scoped 2017 panel on labor-force participation). There was no 2023 panel. SSAB’s own index, updated September 24, 2025, still describes a four-year cadence while its download list stops at 2019; the Board held a “Technical Roundtable” in August 2023 that was not constituted as a panel and produced no report or recommendations. No primary source explains the gap, and the Board’s loss of quorum began in March 2024 — after the missed cycle, so it cannot be the cause.

The panels were advisory in a specific and limited sense: they reviewed the assumptions and methods OCACT uses and recommended improvements. They did not approve the annual assumptions, issue an independent forecast, or score realized outcomes. So the most senior external review layer in the actuarial system evaluates methodology, never accuracy — and as of mid-2026 it has not met in seven years.

4. The scored-forecasting lineage

IARPA ACE and the Good Judgment Project

ACE (2011-2015) ran a multi-team geopolitical forecasting tournament with Brier scoring against resolved outcomes; the Good Judgment Project won it. The peer-reviewed result is Mellers et al., “Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament,” Psychological Science 25(5):1106-1115 (2014): probability training and teaming measurably improved calibration, and the top 2% of Year 1 forecasters, reassembled into elite teams, beat every other condition. Concretely, on questions open at least a month in Year 2, untrained solo forecasters scored Brier .46/.39/.26 across early, middle, and late windows; trained teams .40/.28/.16; superforecasters .25/.19/.07.

Two attribution traps. First, the often-quoted 60% figure — reported as 60% lower error — comes from a companion paper (Tetlock, Mellers, Rohrbaugh & Chen, Current Directions in Psychological Science 23(4):290-295, 2014), and the control was GJP’s own untrained, solo, randomly assigned forecasters — not intelligence analysts. Second, and more important: the famous claim that top forecasters did “about 30 percent better than the average for intelligence community analysts” — who, the same column separately noted, had access to intercepts and secret data — traces to David Ignatius, Washington Post, November 1, 2013, citing “a participant in the project,” with no question set, score definition, sample size, or test. It was never peer-reviewed and must not be attributed to Mellers et al. The closest published comparison (Goldstein et al., MITRE, 2015, public release 15-3175) looked at 139 unclassified questions posted concurrently in 2013-14: mean daily Brier .23 for the IC’s classified-network prediction market versus .21 for GJP’s market (not significantly different), .32 for GJP’s unweighted opinion pool, and .15 for its best research-enhanced aggregation.

And the institutionalization claim needs softening. The scan’s “never institutionalized” is too strong: ODNI’s Office of Analytic Integrity and Standards ran the IC Prediction Market on the classified network from 2010, reaching more than 4,300 cleared users and 190,000 trades under a logarithmic market scoring rule. Real deployment happened. What is absent from the public record is evidence it became routine input to finished intelligence, or that it survives today.

The Hybrid Forecasting Competition

HFC (2017-2020) tested human-machine hybrid forecasting, with SAGE (USC-ISI) as a fielded system. The public record contains a tension worth reporting rather than resolving: IARPA’s own retrospective records completion in May 2020 and lists a 10% improvement over human-only systems as an accomplishment, while then-IARPA Director Catherine Marsh said publicly in September 2020 that HFC was not meeting its metrics and the agency closed it. Both can be true — a performer can beat one comparator while the program misses more demanding targets. No public source names a transition customer or standing deployment.

DARPA SCORE

SCORE (2019-2022) built prediction markets, surveys, and algorithms to forecast whether social and behavioral science claims would replicate, at a scale of thousands of claims, with the Center for Open Science running the evidence infrastructure. DARPA records the program as complete; COS puts the funding end at 2022. The registry, datasets, software, and methods survived, and COS continued the line of work under philanthropic funding (Robert Wood Johnson Foundation) and a public replication-prediction challenge. No operational DoD owner, recurring appropriation, or production interface after SCORE is publicly evidenced.

The pattern across all three, at the strength the sources actually support: federal R&D repeatedly tested scored forecasting and got heterogeneous results. ACE is the clearest success. HFC’s published gain over a recruited-crowd comparator was modest and did not isolate the machine contribution, and IARPA’s director said it missed program metrics. SCORE’s human methods showed signal on a limited matched set while its automated objective fell short. Deployment did happen in places (the IC Prediction Market). What none of them produced was standing, funded, publicly accountable infrastructure outliving its program office.

INFER and its successors

The scan’s “then it stalled” framing is wrong for this one program — the correction matters. The lineage runs unbroken: CSET’s Foretell launched June 2020 at Georgetown as “a variation on a proposal by Philip E. Tetlock”; in January 2022 it became INFER (“INtegrated Forecasting and Estimates of Risk”), run by ARLIS — a DoD University Affiliated Research Center at the University of Maryland — with Cultivate Labs as the platform vendor and seed funding from an Open Philanthropy grant (listed at $7,993,780 over two years); in February 2024 it transferred to RAND and relaunched that September as the RAND Forecasting Initiative, which is live today with active questions (infer-pub.com now 301-redirects to randforecastinginitiative.org).

The mechanics are the ACE inheritance at modest scale: crowd forecasts on admin-resolved questions (“The RFI Team has sole discretion” on resolution), scored with “the Brier Score” and a relative “Accuracy Score” on public leaderboards. Published scale: 33,000+ forecasts across 150+ questions and ~2,500 users in 2022; 124 questions and 32,565 forecasts in 2024. Its About page says it supports policymakers in “the Intelligence Community, the Department of Defense, and the Department of State” — but no primary source names an agency acting on a specific forecast, and no RFI-specific federal award was identified. (A name-only USAspending query understates the vendor’s federal footprint: Cultivate Labs is a DBA of FlyoverWorks, Inc., which holds federal forecasting-software awards including a DTRA contract.) The accountability rails the US government uses here were philanthropically seeded and have no continuing federal owner or appropriation, sustained by a think tank rather than a statute. Its UK counterpart, Cosmic Bazaar, is run inside government by the Professional Head of Intelligence Assessment — the two are described by the program itself as the largest running forecasting efforts supporting their respective governments.

CDC forecast hubs and the weighted interval score

This is the closest structural kin to Thesis anywhere in or near government, and the comparison rewards precision.

The COVID-19 Forecast Hub. Built by the Reich Lab at UMass Amherst with CDC in spring 2020, the hub collected standardized probabilistic submissions on a weekly cycle — 23 quantiles for deaths and hospitalizations (seven for case forecasts, a detail worth keeping straight). The ensemble began as an arithmetic average, moved to an equally weighted median in July 2020, and adopted a weighted median for death forecasts in November 2021. Its definitive evaluation is Cramer et al., “Evaluation of individual and ensemble probabilistic forecasts of COVID-19 mortality in the United States,” PNAS 119(15) e2113561119 (2022), covering 28 entries over 79 forecast weeks: the ensemble scored a relative weighted interval score of 0.61 — 39% less probabilistic error than the baseline — and was the only model to beat the baseline in every location, while 18 of 27 non-baseline entries beat that simple baseline overall and across models, average absolute WIS ran about 76% higher at four weeks than at one week. Operations wound down by target (case and death ensembles in early 2023, the hospitalization hub in May 2024), and a CDC-led successor launched in November 2024 that remains active as of July 2026, with Wednesday-evening submission deadlines and WIS specified for quantile evaluation.

FluSight. CDC’s influenza exercise began as a $75,000 prize competition under 15 U.S.C. §3719 (78 Fed. Reg. 70,303, Nov. 25, 2013) with nine biweekly submissions, not the weekly quantile hub it is now. The scoring history is not one clean regime — and the correction matters for anyone citing it as a proper-scoring precedent: through 2019-20 FluSight used a truncated modified log score (floored at −10), which is not proper as implemented; the 23-quantile WIS era began in 2021-22. FluSight ran through the 2025-26 season despite the year’s CDC disruptions, publishing its final forecast June 11, 2026 with 26 modeling groups participating. Season evaluations are public and retrospective, with lags from about three months to eleven; no 2025-26 evaluation had appeared as of July 27, 2026.

Why WIS matters. The weighted interval score (Bracher, Ray, Gneiting & Reich, “Evaluating epidemic forecasts in an interval format,” PLOS Computational Biology 17(2) e1008618, 2021 — note the author order) is a quantile-based approximation to CRPS, and with the hubs’ 23 quantiles the approximation is close. Both belong to the family of proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery, JASA 102(477):359-378, 2007): a forecaster maximizes expected score by reporting their actual belief, so honesty and overconfidence stop trading off. This is the property that separates the hubs — and Thesis — from every point-error retrospective in sections 1 through 3. It is worth being precise about what propriety does not buy: it does not make any single forecast right, does not fix revised surveillance data, and does not guarantee calibration in small samples.

Custody, honestly characterized. Hub submissions arrive as public pull requests against a hard deadline, validated automatically, preserved in commit history and public mirrors. That is genuine prospective chronology — far more than a private spreadsheet, and it makes silent retrospective editing detectable. But git author dates are client-supplied and repository administrators can rewrite history; no hub source claims an external timestamping authority or immutable escrow. The accurate formulation is deadline-bound public submission with versioned tamper-evidence, not independently witnessed custody — which is precisely the seam where Thesis’s RFC 3161 witnessing differs, and the difference should be argued at that resolution rather than as “no pre-registration.”

The in-government exemplar: weather

The Brier score is a Weather Bureau invention: Glenn W. Brier, “Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability,” Monthly Weather Review 78(1):1-3 (1950), which uses rain/no-rain as its running example. Probability verification originated there, current NWS directives mandate evaluation, and NHC has published annual verification for decades:

  • Policy requires it. NWS Policy Directive 10-16, “NWS Performance and Evaluation” (Feb. 2, 2021) and NWS Instruction 10-1601, “Verification” (July 7, 2022) direct routine matching of forecasts against observations, say that verification statistics alone must not be used to rate an individual forecaster, and define the attributes evaluated — reliability, resolution, sharpness, bias, discrimination, accuracy. Notably, the instruction also bars using verification statistics to rate an individual forecaster.
  • Probabilities are scored properly. Precipitation-probability forecasts are verified with the binary (“half-”) Brier score and published as reliability statistics local offices explain in plain language: among forecasts labeled 30%, did rain fall about 30% of the time?
  • Hurricane forecasts get an annual public report card. The National Hurricane Center has published forecast verification reports since 1968; the 2025-season report (Cangialosi, Martinez, Reinhart & Papin, March 30, 2026) came 120 days after the season ended. On NHC’s homogeneous Atlantic series excluding tropical depressions, mean 48-hour track error fell from 252.5 nautical miles in the 1970s to 55.2 in 2020-2025 — a 78% reduction, with 72-hour error down 77.5%. NHC also publishes reliability diagrams for its probabilistic genesis forecasts.

Two honest qualifications for anyone quoting this contrast. First, the famous track-error improvement is a deterministic distance metric, not a proper probabilistic score — cite it as measured skill improvement, not as evidence that calibrated probabilities improved 78%. Second, “the one domain where government forecasts probabilistically” overstates it: CDC’s hubs do exactly that too, NWS’s public verification is federated across programs rather than consolidated in a single leaderboard, and part of the enterprise verification viewer requires federal credentials. The defensible version is about continuity and routine: probability verification began at the Weather Bureau in 1950, current directives mandate evaluation, and NHC has published a seasonal verification report for decades — while no comparable long-running regime was located among the fiscal institutions or the sampled workload and caseload products.

5. The self-assessing outliers: EIA and USDA

The scan named these two as the best-practice fringe inside government. One holds up better than the other, and the distinction is instructive.

USDA’s WASDE publishes its own error distribution. The July 10, 2026 edition (WASDE-673) carries, on report page 35, a table titled Reliability of July Projections that states its own historical accuracy in plain language: for world wheat production, “chances are 2 out of 3 that the current forecast will not be above or below the final estimate by more than 2.7 percent,” with a USDA-labeled “90 percent confidence interval” of 4.6 percent, an average absolute difference of 14.2 million metric tons, the smallest and largest historical misses, and the directional count — 27 years below the final estimate, 18 above, over 1981/82-2025/26. WASDE and NASS’s Crop Production report (whose July 2026 edition carries a parallel “Reliability of July 1 Crop Production Forecasts” table) are the notable USDA products printing historical error distributions inside the recurring forecast document itself.

Two qualifications keep it honest. The interval is computed from prior point-forecast errors, not elicited as a predictive distribution and then scored for coverage — so it is a historical-error band wearing a confidence-interval label, not a proper score. And “final” generally means USDA’s own later estimate (the first November estimate after the marketing year), so the truth target is a subsequent agency number, revisable, rather than an independent observation.

EIA’s current STEO practice is narrower. STEO is monthly with a 13-24 month horizon, and EIA genuinely did publish comprehensive vintage-by-vintage error tables — in the Short-Term Energy Outlook Annual Supplement 1995, whose Appendix B compared 21 quarterly forecasts across six vintages with average absolute percent errors by target quarter (13.8% overall on imported crude acquisition cost; 3.7% on retail gasoline). No current successor to that whole-product scorecard was located: today’s supplements page offers narrower one-off lookbacks and model-module backtests, and backtests that substitute realized explanatory variables do not test the forecast as issued. EIA’s NYMEX-options-implied price bands — which bracketed futures prices, not EIA’s own forecast — were published through September 2025, suspended in October 2025, and had not returned by July 2026. On the AEO side the retrospective tradition continues: AEO 2025 and AEO 2026 both appeared, and the latest retrospective review was released March 10, 2026.

So the corrected claim is: USDA publishes a recurring accuracy self-assessment inside its recurring forecast; EIA has published excellent ones historically but no current recurring whole-product equivalent was found. Neither uses proper scoring; neither pre-registers or seals a vintage.

6. Regulatory analysis and the retrospective-review gap

Significant federal rules ship with an assessment of costs and benefits, quantified where feasible. Almost none is ever checked against what happened.

State the legal position precisely, because the loose version is easy to refute: EO 12866 — not Circular A-4 — creates the duty, requiring an assessment for covered significant regulatory actions, subject to OIRA waiver, with more detailed benefit-cost analysis for §3(f)(1) actions and quantification required only “to the extent feasible.” Circular A-4 supplies the methodology, not the mandate.

What governs today. OMB Circular A-4 supplies the methodology for the prospective benefit-cost analysis EO 12866 requires. The November 9, 2023 revision modernized it — a 2.0% default social discount rate for the first 30 years, expanded treatment of uncertainty and distributional analysis — and was then revoked: Executive Order 14192, “Unleashing Prosperity Through Deregulation” (January 31, 2025; 90 Fed. Reg. 9065), §6(b) directed OMB to reinstate the 2003 circular, which OMB executed by memorandum M-25-15 on February 12, 2025. The operative guidance in mid-2026 is the September 2003 version.

Retrospective review is formally required. EO 12866 §5 (1993), EO 13563 §6 (2011), and EO 13610 (2012) all direct agencies to review existing rules. The question is whether any of that review tests the original numbers.

The evidence that it doesn’t, stated carefully. The strongest quantitative finding is GAO-07-791, Reexamining Regulations: Opportunities Exist to Improve Effectiveness and Transparency of Retrospective Reviews (July 16, 2007): nine agencies reported more than 1,300 reviews performed in 2001-2006, and in GAO’s document review “we only identified two reviews that focused on this purpose” — the purpose being testing the validity of the original benefit and cost estimates. Quote it with its own caveat attached: GAO described its document review as limited, and the 1,300 figure is agency-reported, so “two out of 1,300” is not an exhaustively coded ratio. It remains the best available evidence that ex-post validation is rare.

ACUS reached the institutional version of the same conclusion in Recommendation 2014-5, Retrospective Review of Agency Rules (adopted December 4, 2014; 79 Fed. Reg. 75114): “retrospective review of regulations has not been held to the same standard as prospective review.” ACUS did not say review never happens — it said the incentives are weak, review risks becoming pro forma without senior commitment, and a durable evaluation-and-improvement culture is not yet part of most regulatory programs.

The later GAO-14-268 (April 11, 2014) is frequently miscited in this context, so: it found 22 executive agencies reporting 246 completed analyses with final actions, of which only 38 quantified expected savings using inconsistent methods — but it did not test whether projected savings materialized, and it should not be credited with the “two reviews” finding.

The net picture: the government requires benefit-cost assessment for covered significant actions (subject to waiver, quantified where feasible), requires periodic review of rules, and has no systematic mechanism connecting the two. This review found no government-wide routine system linking original regulatory estimates to realized outcomes, though individual validations exist — which is the claim the evidence supports, and is weaker than “nobody checks.”

7. An adjacent opening: the FY27 NDAA’s acquisition policy experimentation laboratory

A House committee moved this year to expand an acquisition-specific policy-modeling capability. Read narrowly — which is the only way it should be read — it shows one committee’s interest in building simulation capacity it does not currently have. It does not establish the absence of related capabilities across government, does not endorse proper scoring or forecast registries, and is not a validation of any particular architecture.

The document. National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2027, H.R. Rep. No. 119-698, 119th Cong., 2d sess. (June 15, 2026) — the House Armed Services Committee report accompanying H.R. 8800. The directive appears under Title XVIII, “Revitalization of the Defense Industrial Base,” as an Item of Special Interest headed “An Enduring Acquisition Policy Experimentation Laboratory to Model, Simulate, and Analyze the Impact of Acquisition and Industrial Base Policies,” at printed pages 449-450 (PDF pages 491-492 of the authenticated GPO file).

The operative language, quoted exactly:

Therefore, the committee directs the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment, leveraging the Acquisition Innovation Research Center (AIRC) established under section 4142 of title 10, United States Code, to develop an initial but enduring Acquisition Policy Experimentation Laboratory (APEL) not later than January 15, 2027.

The three initial objectives are to model, simulate, and analyze policy options that “(1) create the right industrial structure; (2) incentivize contractors of the Department to maintain and invest in increasing the capability and capacity of the industrial base; and (3) remove barriers to entry, reduce burden on contractors, and increase defense industrial base capability and capacity.” A briefing to HASC is due December 1, 2026, covering designation of a research team, “initial policies or policy proposals to model,” development timelines, and estimated annual funding.

Four qualifications. The pagination discrepancy in earlier notes is resolved and both numbers were right about different documents: printed pp. 449-450 is the published committee report; “p. 493” is the PDF-viewer page of the pre-markup Chairman’s Mark (its printed p. 492). This is committee-report language, not bill text — a full-text check of the reported H.R. 8800 finds no occurrence of “Acquisition Policy Experimentation Laboratory” or “APEL” — so it carries oversight weight, not statutory force. It is not yet law: the House passed H.R. 8800 on July 22, 2026 by 216-212, while a Senate cloture motion on its own FY2027 NDAA (S. 4784) failed 50-46 on July 14, so the FY2027 NDAA has passed neither both chambers nor conference. And a policy-simulation laboratory is a different object from an ex-post forecast-evaluation layer: APEL is directed to model and analyze policy options, not to score anyone’s past forecasts. Treat it as a live opportunity in an adjacent domain, not as evidence for the report’s general thesis.

8. The operational layer: how agencies forecast themselves

The scorekeepers above are the visible tip. Beneath them, program agencies and sub-federal governments forecast their own workloads, caseloads, and finances constantly. This is the layer closest to where Thesis’s registry lives, and its accountability practices are uneven: IRS and FAA publish numerical retrospectives, while for other sampled products no evaluation was located. Tested claim by claim below rather than asserted.

Federal program agencies

The blunt version of the wedge — “agencies never score themselves” — is false, and the report is stronger for saying so. Several operational agencies publish their own numerical accuracy tables:

  • IRS is the strongest counterexample. All three return-projection publications carry accuracy tables. Publication 6292 (Fiscal Year Return Projections for the United States: 2025-2032, Rev. 9-2025) prints Table 3, “Accuracy Measures for U.S. Forecasts of Major Return Categories — Mean Absolute Percent Error (MAPE) and Number of Overprojections for the Four Most Recent Projection Cycles,” grouping errors by one- through five-year horizons. Projections are produced semiannually and feed workload and staffing plans.
  • FAA publishes Appendix B, “FAA Forecast Accuracy,” in the Aerospace Forecast Fiscal Years 2026-2046, reporting MAPE at one- to five-year horizons across six measures: passenger enplanements degrade from 0.7% error at one year to 9.2% at five; available seat miles from 1.0% to 10.4%; real GDP from 1.2% to 7.5%.
  • CMS/OACT has a systematic multi-vintage study of health-expenditure projection accuracy that includes Medicaid, and Census has published projection-error studies — though the most recent located date from 2002.

Against these, SSA’s disability and hearings workloads have public numerical evaluations only from GAO, the OIG, or outside researchers. VA needs a finer distinction: GAO documented budget misses and process weaknesses but expressly did not isolate the Enrollee Health Care Projection Model’s output accuracy, VA reports internal actual-to-expected validation, and no public EHCPM error series was located; VBA claims workload is a separate product with external-only evaluation. FNS is the clearest available-but-unused case: SNAP participation is projected annually for the budget and published monthly as actuals — the resolution data already exists, and no evaluation of the projections against it was found. (One correction worth carrying: DOL/ETA’s “State Unemployment Insurance Trust Fund Solvency Report” is an as-of measurement of the Average High Cost Multiple, not a forecast product, so it does not belong on a list of unscored forecasts.)

Three observations refine rather than rescue the original claim:

  1. None of it is proper scoring. Every self-assessment found is point-error — MAPE, signed percentage error, revision comparison, narrative attribution. These are Type B products evaluated with Type B metrics, which is internally consistent; what is absent is any elicited distribution that could be checked for calibration. Where agencies publish alternative scenarios (Census migration variants, FAA optimistic/pessimistic cases, VA sensitivities) they carry no probabilities by design.
  2. Evaluation windows involve disclosed judgment. FAA’s accuracy appendix excludes forecasts made for FY2020-FY2022 because COVID-19’s “unprecedented impacts” could make inferences about its current method inconclusive. That is a defensible and disclosed methodological choice — excluding the shock answers a question about the current method, including it answers a question about total historical performance. Stronger practice would publish both, or set exception rules prospectively. Discretion exercised in the open is not cherry-picking, and Thesis exercises its own discretion one level earlier, in choosing which targets enter the registry at all.
  3. Vintages are archived, not sealed. Prior editions remain downloadable at stable URLs; none of these programs describes pre-registration, cryptographic hashing, or immutable custody. Public archive, not tamper-evident registry — a difference in the strength of the chronology guarantee, not an allegation about anyone’s conduct.

The weather exception

NOAA/NWS is the operational layer’s most mature accountability loop — mandated verification, properly scored precipitation probabilities, and an annual public hurricane report card. It is covered in full in section 4 above, alongside the other properly scored systems, because methodologically it belongs with the CDC hubs rather than with the agency workload projections here.

State governments

The states are more varied than any federal generalization, and several are ahead of Washington on transparency about their own errors.

Revenue forecasting. Washington State’s Economic and Revenue Forecast Council — an independent agency outside both branches, forecasting quarterly under RCW 82.33.020 with statutory optimistic and pessimistic alternatives — publishes monthly forecast-versus-collection variance and updates a “Track Record” section in every quarterly forecast (November 2025 edition, Vol. LIX No. 4). Florida, Utah (under Utah Code §36-12-13(2)(a)), and California’s Department of Finance and LAO all publish some form of signed forecast error or periodic backtest. Michigan, whose January and May consensus conferences are among the most cited, publishes dated conference documents but no accuracy evaluation that this review located.

External evaluation supplies the cross-state comparisons the states don’t produce about each other: Pew’s revenue-volatility work provides context rather than a numerical error panel, and the Rockefeller Institute’s accuracy studies rest on error data supplied by state budget offices themselves.

Corrections populations produce another counterexample. Virginia’s appropriation act (Chapter 725, Item 377) requires annually revised six-year adult and juvenile offender-population forecasts by October 15, built by a consensus process with a Technical Advisory Committee, independent comparator forecasts from the Department of Planning and Budget, and a Policy Committee that selects the final number. The annual report both validates candidate models and prints prior forecast-versus-actual comparisons — a direct counterexample to the claim that corrections-forecast accuracy surfaces only in outside audits.

Medicaid caseloads are mixed across the sampled states, with no prevalence established: Washington’s forecasts drew an external legislative audit alongside unpublished internal monitoring, while Oregon’s health agency publishes its own multi-vintage monthly error files.

Public pension assumed returns are the clearest case of external-only evaluation at the plan level: the assumption is set by the board on the actuary’s advice, and the comparison of assumed against realized returns is made by outside analysts.

Local and infrastructure

This layer has the best-documented forecast bias in the report, and also the most developed accountability structure.

Transit’s before-and-after regime is the strongest institutional design in this report. Under 49 C.F.R. §611.211, sponsors of major capital projects under a full-funding grant agreement must preserve their forecasts made at defined decision milestones, collect outcomes two years after opening, compare predicted against actual scope, capital cost, service, operating cost, and ridership, and file a final report within three years of opening — with grant advancement and closeout as leverage. Forecasts are archived at the decision point and resolved later against measured outcomes: that is functionally pre-registration, imposed by a funding condition. FTA reviews and publishes the individual studies and periodic aggregates (Predicted Versus Actual Impacts of Capital Investment Grants Projects — 2020, covering 29 projects opened 2007-2015). No annual report to Congress after the eighth edition (March 2017) was located; the IIJA subsequently eliminated the annual duty in 2021 and established biennial GAO review, so the reporting weakened even as the underlying discipline continued.

The bias is large historically and smaller in the current cohort — report both. In FTA’s own 2020 study of 29 projects opened 2007-2015, 13 of 27 ridership forecasts landed within ±20%, with mean actual/forecast of 92% and median 90% — though 11 of the 14 material misses were over-forecasts. The classic studies describe a much larger historical bias: Pickrell’s Urban Rail Transit Projects: Forecast Versus Actual Ridership and Costs (USDOT DOT-T-91-04, October 1990) found all nine comparable projects fell below forecast, by 28% to 85%, with only Washington carrying more than half its forecast. Flyvbjerg, Skamris Holm & Buhl, “How (In)accurate Are Demand Forecasts in Public Works Projects?” JAPA 71(2):131-146 (2005), covering 210 projects in 14 countries, found nine in ten rail forecasts overestimated demand, with forecast demand averaging 105.6% above actual across 25 rail projects after excluding two outliers. State the denominator when quoting this: “forecast 105.6% above actual” is not “actual 105.6% below forecast.”

Toll roads repeat the pattern with an outside evaluator: the benchmark study is Bain & Polakovic, Traffic Forecasting Risk Study Update 2005 (Standard & Poor’s, August 25, 2005) — produced by a credit-rating agency pricing the risk, not by the forecasters or authorities that made the original estimates.

What survives is a statement about kind rather than existence. Across the self-assessments found in this operational layer — federal, state, and local, excluding the weather and outbreak systems in section 4 — none uses a proper scoring rule. They report mean absolute percentage error, signed differentials, RMSE-derived bands, revision comparisons, and narrative attribution. None publishes an elicited predictive distribution that could be tested for calibration, so overconfidence is not detectable from anything they publish. (PBGC is the near-miss: it produces genuine stochastic distributions, and external reviewers have compared mature vintages against outcomes, but its own peer reviews still report no formal backtesting process.) Three further structural features recur:

  1. Evaluation design carries disclosed judgment — sometimes. FAA excludes the COVID vintages; the OBR substitutes a scenario for the March 2020 EFO; WASDE’s “final” is USDA’s own later estimate. Each is reasonable and each is a choice made at analysis time. The counterexamples matter too: SSA’s Table III.A4 compares a fixed set of prior report vintages every year, and FTA’s grant conditions fix the comparison before the project opens. Ex-post window choice is common, not universal.
  2. Vintages are archived, not sealed. Publication chronology rests on dated releases and wide dissemination rather than on cryptographic evidence. This review found no case of a fiscal forecast being retrospectively altered, and does not allege one.
  3. Coverage is selective by necessity. Agencies evaluate a subset of what they publish, chosen by relevance and data availability.

So the defensible statement is not that government fails to check itself. It is: where outcomes are observable, evaluation is uneven and relies on metrics that cannot detect overconfidence — which leaves the specific question of calibration unanswered across the fiscal and program landscape, outside the weather and outbreak systems that answer it routinely.

9. Thesis today, from its public surfaces

Everything in this section was read on 2026-07-30 from app.thesisinstitute.org or the public ThesisInstitute/thesis repository, including artifact-level inspection of the custody chain.

What it is. A registry of automatically resolvable public-data targets with agent-generated probabilistic forecasts, scored on resolution. The target architecture lists 750 targets over 750 source series with 2,620 observations, spanning BLS (150 targets: OEWS occupational employment, Employment Projections, the Employment Situation), USDA FNS (165: SNAP payment error rates and application timeliness), CMS (107: Medicaid/CHIP eligibility processing and enrollment), BEA personal income (11), IRS filing-season statistics (5), and Eurostat unemployment (4). The Thesis Log records 1,188 recorded agent runs, each emitting a 201-point CDF against a prediction spec.

Pre-registration and resolution — with the coverage stated honestly. The spawned-cell contract (docs/cell-contract.md) requires each forecast it governs to ship with a resolution-grade question (“exact series, period, adjustment, first print”), a resolutionRule fixing the exact table, line, rounding, and condition policy, a resolutionDate verified from the official release calendar, and a dataPointId pinning the vintage (…first_print). Sources that revise continuously get a registered-query snapshot policy instead: the outcome is whatever the pinned query returns on the registered capture date, with response bytes archived.

Two coverage facts belong next to that description, because the contract governs a subset of the catalog rather than all of it:

  • Prospective target registration covers a minority of the registry. The canonical registration snapshots in records/targets/ cover 95 unique dataPointIds across 100 files, while the generated ledger labels 379 entries “Backfilled target registration” against a catalog of 750 targets. A backfilled registration is not evidence that the rule was fixed before the forecast. The defensible claim is that current scheduled-docket runs commit a canonical registration before the agent executes — not that all 750 targets carry pre-run registration evidence.
  • Full activity logs cover 351 of 1,188 runs. In the July 30 witnessed export, 379 of 1,188 recorded runs carry any activityLog, and 351 carry the full prompt / raw-response / parsed-cell / validation-report set. CI enforces trace depth (≥7 reasoning steps, ≥3 tool calls carrying numbers fetched in-run, an explicit base rate, a math derivation, a disconfirming consideration) on spawned thesis.analyst runs; legacy, prototype, and comparison runs do not all satisfy it.

Custody. Records are append-only under records/ in the public repository, and the record chain is externally witnessed by two independent RFC 3161 timestamp authorities, DigiCert and FreeTSA (.tsr token files, verified by artifact inspection; latest batch 2026-07-30). Dual witnessing is not a historical invariant: current v2 batches and all four headline-score roots carry two authorities, while 14 older batches carry one and one receipt is unavailable. The calibration page defines the headline tier accordingly: a run’s score counts as witness-verified only when its sealed custody root carries an RFC 3161 timestamp in the public record chain before the observation existed. Runs with merely recorded timestamps are published as a separate claimed-time tier below the fold; runs whose chronology cannot be verified are excluded and counted. Ed25519 producer signing was activated by a trust-root ceremony on 2026-07-21; snapshots after the activation boundary require a valid producer signature, and fourteen .producer.sig files are present through July 30 (scripts/producer_signing_pins.py).

Scoring. CRPS (normalized, and as a ratio against a persistence baseline), 80%-interval coverage, and PIT histograms, computed per forecaster and published on the calibration page with the raw score rows.

The scoreboard. These figures move as cells resolve; read them with the timestamp attached. As of the evening of 2026-07-30 the witness-verified headline shows 8 scored forecasts, 3 of them inside their stated 80% intervals — 38% coverage against an 80% target — with unpaired mean normalized CRPS of 2.350 (a figure summarizing the 2 of 8 rows that carry a registered normalization scale) and a CRPS ratio of 1.18 against persistence across the two matched targets: the agent is currently behind the persistence baseline, winning one comparison of two. Behind it, the published-chronology tier holds 206 scores at 75% coverage (155 of 206; 198 of them claimed-time-only), with zero chronology violations, and 920 runs await resolution. Over the four days this report was being verified, the witnessed tier moved from 2 scores at 0% coverage to 4 at 50% to 8 at 38%, and the persistence ratio flipped from 0.95 to 1.18. Every one of those readings was accurate at its timestamp, which is the point of a scoreboard that updates itself rather than being written up.

What those numbers do and do not support. They establish that the system fixes chronology and publishes outcomes — favorable and adverse — without an intervening editorial decision. They establish nothing about calibration or skill. Eight observations support no inference in either direction; 38% observed coverage against an 80% target would indicate intervals that are far too narrow if it held up, and at n=8 it is weak evidence at best. The persistence comparison rests on two matched targets and currently runs against the agent. The 198 claimed-time-only scores sit in a separate, weaker tier and are likely correlated across series, horizons, and a common macro episode, so the run count overstates effective sample size. No comparative claim in this report rests on any of it.

Conditional cells. The cell contract types every forecast data, policy, or conditional, with conditionalOn binding a checkable condition to a provision reference; the homepage describes the three-type design (data points, policy states, conditionals). The conditional layer is young — few cells, none yet in the witnessed scored set.

11. Five defensible claims

Each is stated at the strength the evidence supports, with its citation and its limiting qualifier.

1. Neither CBO nor JCT publishes a standing program of provision-level retrospectives, and JCT says so in its own methodology document. CBO’s accuracy report states that it adjusts baselines using original cost estimates so “any errors in the initial cost estimates are reflected in the projection errors discussed in this report” — carried into aggregates rather than attributed per bill (The Accuracy of CBO’s Budget Projections for Fiscal Year 2025, January 16, 2026). JCT’s methodology publication says “there is no formal process for the revisiting of estimates over time” (JCX-46-11, pp. 6-7). Qualifier — and it is a large one: cost estimates are Type C conditional estimates whose counterfactual is never observed, so most whole-bill effects cannot be resolved even in principle. CBO’s aggregate self-evaluation is genuinely strong. The claim is about the absence of a public record of component-level checks, not about competence or candor.

2. In fiscal, macroeconomic, and sampled operational forecasting, no standing published evaluation uses a proper scoring rule — while two other corners of the same government do. Across CBO, JCT, OMB, OTA, the Fed, the IMF, OBR, SSA, CMS, IRS, FAA, NCES, WASDE, EIA, and the states, every published accuracy metric is point-error or narrative. Meanwhile NWS verifies precipitation probabilities with the Brier score (Brier 1950) and CDC’s hubs score quantile submissions with the weighted interval score (Bracher, Ray, Gneiting & Reich 2021). The finding is a contrast within government, not a charge against it. Qualifiers: it is an absence claim resting on documented searches, so speak it as “no standing published program found”; and for Type C conditional estimates the absence is appropriate rather than a deficiency.

3. No standing ex-post scoring of the IMF WEO, Fed SEP, or OBR probability bands was located. The IMF’s April 2026 WEO carries explicit tail probabilities (a 25% chance global growth falls below 2%); the Fed and the OBR publish fan charts. These are Type A products, fully scoreable, and no standing ex-post scoring of any of them was located. The OBR — the most self-critical institution in this review — made isolated outturn-versus-band observations in two located editions (2016 FER; 2024 FER Box 2.1), with no recurring coverage series found. Qualifier: SSA’s low- and high-cost alternatives are Type D scenarios that were never probability intervals, and this claim excludes them; the point stands on the IMF, Fed, and OBR bands alone.

4. Publication chronology across the landscape rests on informal safeguards, not on evidence. Agency vintages are archived and widely mirrored, which makes undisclosed alteration risky and usually detectable — but nothing is cryptographically bound to a publication time, and version discipline varies (CMS’s November 2020 accuracy analysis occupies the same ProjectionAccuracy.pdf URL that its own footnote assigns to a February 2018 predecessor). Thesis’s record chain is timestamped by two independent RFC 3161 authorities, and its headline tier admits only scores whose custody root was witnessed before the observation existed. Qualifiers, both material: this review documents no case of a fiscal forecast being retrospectively altered, so the claim is about the strength of the guarantee, not about misconduct; and an RFC 3161 token attests only that certain bytes existed by a certain time — it says nothing about whether the target mattered, the resolution rule was fair, the scoring code was right, or the submitted universe was complete. The CDC hubs’ deadline-bound public git submissions are already meaningfully prospective, so the real comparison is external witnessing versus administrator-controlled version history.

5. The operational layer resolves against observable outcomes and still goes unscored — including where the agency already publishes the resolution data. This is the cleanest case in the report because it involves no counterfactuals: program agencies forecast Type B quantities that are later published as actuals. Several print MAPE tables (IRS Pub. 6292 Table 3; FAA Appendix B; NCES; WASDE). None elicits or evaluates a distribution. And FNS projects SNAP participation annually for the budget while publishing actual participation monthly, with no evaluation of the projections against it located in this review. Qualifier: practice across this layer is uneven rather than uniformly absent, and no comparative measure supports calling it the “least” accountable layer — the defensible claim is that scoring is available here at low cost and is not being done.

12. What Thesis does not do better

These bound every claim above.

Authority. CBO, JCT, OBR, and the Trustees produce numbers with legal and procedural standing: CBO and JCT estimates feed the Budget Committee determinations that govern Budget Act points of order; an OBR forecast determines whether a fiscal rule is met. Thesis produces evidence about forecast quality. Nothing in the architecture confers scorekeeping authority, and no amount of calibration would.

Legal conditionality. A cost estimate is a conditional statement about a specific legislative text under baseline conventions that are themselves statutory — the >$50 million expiring-programs rule, full funding of entitlement authority, the 50% weighting of pending rules. Encoding that correctly is most of the work, and CBO and JCT have decades of institutional practice in it. Thesis’s conditional cells are young: few in number, none yet in the witnessed scored set.

Long-horizon actuarial depth. OCACT and CMS OACT project 75 years with mortality, fertility, immigration, disability incidence, and health cost growth models refined over decades and reviewed by external technical panels. Most Thesis targets are short-horizon, though the registry includes multi-year targets resolving as late as 2035; nothing in it approaches a 75-year actuarial model. The long-horizon critique here is narrow — no recurring agency-run score of 75-year solvency projections was found, and PBGC’s externally reviewed distributions show even that gap is not absolute — and it is emphatically not a claim that Thesis could produce such projections.

Track record length. The witnessed headline stands at 8 scored forecasts, 5 of them outside their 80% intervals, with the agent currently behind the persistence baseline on the two matched targets. CBO publishes error statistics spanning 1985-2024. The architecture claim is supported by a track record eight data points long, and “come back when you have a hundred” is a fair response.

The architecture does not yet cover its own catalog. The claim that resolution rules are fixed before the run holds for current scheduled-docket runs, not for the whole registry: 94 targets carry canonical prospective registration snapshots while 379 ledger entries are labeled “Backfilled target registration,” against 750 catalog targets. Full artifact traces exist for 351 of 1,188 runs. Any comparison that contrasts an idealized Thesis contract with agency practice is comparing a policy to a record, and the record is partial.

Independence — the objection that matters most. Thesis currently scores its own agent-generated forecasts. It is a self-scoring forecaster, not yet an evaluator of anyone else’s work: nothing here constitutes a scoreboard of issued CBO, JCT, Fed, OBR, SSA, or CMS forecasts. Calling it an “external evaluation layer” describes an aspiration. And automation is not independence — Thesis selects the target universe, horizons, forecasters, resolution rules, the first-print convention, baselines, normalization, scoring code, and publication framing. That is the same self-evaluation conflict the report identifies in agencies, with less statutory oversight attached.

What would have to exist before the scoreboard deserves weight. A pre-registered target universe with an inclusion rule that keeps every failed, dropped, and non-resolving run in view; a witnessed cohort large enough to report effective sample size with dependence clustered by series and horizon; matched head-to-head comparison against official forecasts on identical estimands, information cutoffs, and outcome vintages; strong baselines fixed in advance (seasonal naïve, autoregressive, professional consensus, and the agency forecast where one exists) rather than a single persistence comparison; independent governance for resolution disputes and scoring changes; an external audit of code, data leakage, and timestamps; and sensitivity analysis across first print, a fixed revised vintage, and latest data. None of that exists today.

Breadth of what the institutions actually do. CBO’s cost estimates carry narrative analysis of mechanism, distribution, and implementation risk that no scored point-forecast captures. Scoring is not a substitute for analysis; it is a missing complement.

Proper scoring is not a cure-all. Propriety controls one reporting incentive under standard assumptions — it makes truthful reporting optimal in expectation for an individual forecaster. It does not prevent organizational gaming, guarantee calibration in small or correlated samples, survive nonstationarity, or settle scale and normalization choices. It creates its own Goodhart risks: a coverage-only league table rewards interval widening, and any scored registry rewards choosing targets that resolve cleanly over questions that matter. Thesis’s registry is built from automatically resolvable series, which is exactly the selection pressure to watch.

Evaluation competes for the same scarce hours. Per-provision retrospectives would compete directly with live estimates produced under statutory deadlines, using confidential taxpayer data and confidential Member requests in JCT’s case. The marginal benefit of an evaluation layer has to be weighed against that opportunity cost rather than treated as free.

On the comparison set. The CDC forecast hubs already do much of this — proper scoring, deadline-bound prospective submission, public evaluation, an ensemble that beats its baseline. The accurate framing is not that Thesis invented these rails, but that the hubs demonstrated them for repeated public-health targets and no equivalent was found for fiscal forecasts or the sampled non-weather, non-outbreak workload and caseload products.
